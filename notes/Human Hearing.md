# Chapter 2: Human Hearing

## 2.1.1 Structure of the ear and the hearing process

- The **ear** converts **sound** waves into **electrical** signals sent to the **brain**.
- Three parts: **outer ear** (pinna, ear canal), **middle ear** (eardrum, ossicles), **inner ear** (cochlea).
- Outer and middle ear are filled with **air**. Inner ear is filled with **fluid**.
- The cochlea discriminates **frequencies** of sound.

**Hearing process**

1. Pinna collects sound, ear canal directs it to the **eardrum**.
2. Sound pressure sets the eardrum into **vibration**.
3. Three **ear bones (ossicles)** pass the vibration to the **oval window**.
4. Oval window sets the fluid in the **cochlea** into vibration.
5. **Hair cells** in the cochlea convert the vibration into electrical signals.
6. **Auditory nerve** carries the signals to the brain.

**Pressure amplification (step 3)**

- **Lever action** of the ossicles: force $\times1.3$.
- **Area ratio**: effective area of eardrum is $20$ times that of the oval window, so pressure $\times20$ ($P=\dfrac{F}{A}$).
- Total amplification $=1.3\times20=\mathbf{26}$ times.

**Frequency discrimination (step 4)**

- Different regions of the **basilar membrane** vibrate for different frequencies.
- Near the base (oval window): **high** frequency (up to $20\,000\ \text{Hz}$). Near the apex: **low** frequency (down to $20\ \text{Hz}$).
- The hair cells at that position are stimulated, and the brain finds the frequency from the **position** of the stimulated hair cells.

## 2.1.2 Sound intensity level

- Intensity: $I=\dfrac{\text{energy}}{\text{area}\times\text{time}}=\dfrac{P}{A}$, unit $\text{W m}^{-2}$.
- Point source, power $P$: $I=\dfrac{P}{4\pi r^2}$, so $I\propto\dfrac{1}{r^2}$ (**inverse square law**).
- The ear responds to a **wide** range ($10^{-12}$ to $10^{2}\ \text{W m}^{-2}$), so we use the **decibel** scale:

$$L=10\log\frac{I}{I_0}\ \text{dB}$$

- $I_0=10^{-12}\ \text{W m}^{-2}$ is the **threshold of hearing**.
- $0\ \text{dB}$: softest audible. $60\ \text{dB}$: conversation at $1\ \text{m}$. $120\ \text{dB}$: aircraft at $175\ \text{m}$.
- $I\times10\Rightarrow L+10\ \text{dB}$. $I\times2\Rightarrow L+3\ \text{dB}$.
- **Never add dB directly. Add intensities, then convert.**

### Exercise 1

A loudspeaker produces a sound of intensity level $60\ \text{dB}$ at point P. The background noise is also $60\ \text{dB}$. Estimate the reading of a dB-meter placed at P.

> [!TIP] Answer
> $I_s=I_b$, so combined intensity $=2I_b$. $L=60+10\log2\approx\mathbf{63.0\ dB}$

### Exercise 2

Ray's voice is $40\ \text{dB}$ at Mike's position (intensity $I_1$).
(a) If Ray receives an intensity $2I_1$ from Mike, what is the intensity level of Mike's voice at Ray's position?
(b) If the distance between Ray and Mike is doubled, what is the intensity level of Ray's voice at Mike's position?

> [!TIP] Answer
> (a) $L=40+10\log2\approx\mathbf{43.0\ dB}$
>
> (b) Distance doubled, so intensity becomes $\dfrac{I_1}{4}$: $L=40-10\log4\approx\mathbf{34.0\ dB}$

### Exercise 3

The average sound intensity level of human speech is about $70\ \text{dB}$. When $40$ students talk at the same time, estimate the intensity level.
A. $72\ \text{dB}$  B. $86\ \text{dB}$  C. $88\ \text{dB}$  D. $110\ \text{dB}$

> [!TIP] Answer: **B**
> Intensity $=40I_1$: $L=70+10\log40\approx86\ \text{dB}$

### Exercise 4

If the sound intensity level of a source increases by $6\ \text{dB}$, the power emitted is multiplied by approximately
A. $\log_{10}6$  B. $2$  C. $4$  D. $10^6$

> [!TIP] Answer: **C**
> $6=10\log\dfrac{I_2}{I_1}\Rightarrow\dfrac{I_2}{I_1}\approx4$. Since $P=IA$, power $\times4$.

## 2.1.3 Curves of equal loudness

- **Intensity** is independent of the listener. **Loudness** is a sensation and depends on frequency, intensity and the listener's hearing.
- Same intensity level but different **frequency** gives different loudness.
- Unit: **phon**. A sound of $n$ phons is as loud as a **$1000\ \text{Hz}$** pure note at $n\ \text{dB}$ (e.g. $40\ \text{dB}$ at $1000\ \text{Hz}=40$ phons).
- All sounds on the same curve have the same loudness.
- The ear is most sensitive at about $3000$ to $4000\ \text{Hz}$.
- At very high or very low frequencies a **higher** intensity is needed for the same loudness.
- The ear can hear some sounds below $0\ \text{dB}$.

### Exercise

(a) Find the loudness (in phons) of (i) a $40\ \text{dB}$ sound at $90\ \text{Hz}$, (ii) a $60\ \text{dB}$ sound at $100\ \text{Hz}$.
(b) Find the intensity level of a sound of loudness $20$ phons and frequency $6000\ \text{Hz}$.
(c) Find the ratio of the intensity level of a $60$ phon sound at $50\ \text{Hz}$ to that of a $60$ phon sound at $1000\ \text{Hz}$.
(d) Hence find the ratio of their intensities.

> [!TIP] Answer
> (a) (i) $\mathbf{0}$ phon. (ii) $\mathbf{40}$ phons.
>
> (b) $\mathbf{20\ dB}$
>
> (c) $60$ phons: $80\ \text{dB}$ at $50\ \text{Hz}$, $60\ \text{dB}$ at $1000\ \text{Hz}$. Ratio $=80:60=\mathbf{4:3}$.
>
> (d) $80=10\log\dfrac{I_1}{I_0}$ and $60=10\log\dfrac{I_2}{I_0}$. Subtract: $20=10\log\dfrac{I_1}{I_2}\Rightarrow I_1:I_2=\mathbf{100:1}$.

## 2.1.4 Effect of noise on hearing

- Loud noise damages **hair cells** in the **cochlea**, so signals may not reach the brain: **hearing loss**.
- Effects: **upward** shift in the threshold of hearing, and **decreased** sensitivity at some frequencies (a 'crest' in the equal loudness curve, e.g. machinery workers).
- **Temporary** loss: sensitivity returns to normal after a short time.
- **Permanent** loss: hair cells permanently damaged by prolonged exposure to loud noise.
